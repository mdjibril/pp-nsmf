[0] Privacy-Preserving Network Security Monitoring: A Framework for Balancing Cybersecurity and Data Protection in Nigerian Organisations
[1] Abstract
[2] Network security monitoring enables organisations to identify suspicious activity, investigate incidents and maintain operational security, but the telemetry required for these functions can expose identifiers and communication patterns. This creates a practical challenge for organisations seeking to strengthen cybersecurity while limiting unnecessary exposure of identifying information. This study presents and experimentally evaluates a Privacy-Preserving Network Security Monitoring Framework (PP-NSMF) that combines pseudonymisation, data minimisation and temporal aggregation in a controlled network environment. The framework was implemented using GNS3, Zeek, Suricata and Python-based telemetry transformation and validation procedures. Six synthetic traffic scenarios were examined: HTTP requests, DNS queries, a controlled TCP port scan, repeated authentication failures, periodic TCP connections and synthetic bulk data transfer. The compliant dataset comprised 29 successful runs, with five repetitions per scenario except DNS, for which four compliant runs were retained. Telemetry was examined across four stages: raw collection (C0), pseudonymisation (C1), minimisation with pseudonymisation (C2), and minimisation with pseudonymisation followed by 60-second aggregation (C3). Source-matched Suricata byte-volume reductions at C2 ranged from 85.78% to 98.30% across scenarios, while C3 reductions ranged from 98.47% to 99.23%. Pseudonymisation alone increased telemetry volume in every compliant run because the generated pseudonyms were longer than the original IPv4 identifiers. The implemented validation checks detected no raw identifiers in the tested transformed outputs and reported consistent pseudonym mappings in all 29 runs. These findings demonstrate the technical feasibility of combining pseudonymisation, field minimisation and aggregation in a controlled laboratory setting. They do not establish complete privacy, regulatory compliance, investigation utility or general intrusion-detection effectiveness. The study contributes an auditable experimental approach for examining telemetry transformation and the trade-offs between identifier protection and monitoring-data volume.

[7] 1. Introduction
[8] Network logs and traffic-analysis records allow administrators to identify suspicious connections, investigate incidents, understand protocol behaviour and reconstruct events. The same records may also contain source and destination IP addresses, hostnames, queried domains, timestamps, connection identifiers and other information that permits activities to be linked across time. Monitoring therefore creates a tension: organisations need sufficient evidence to detect and investigate threats, yet retaining detailed telemetry can increase the amount of information exposed through unauthorised access, misuse or inappropriate retention.
[9] The privacy implications of network telemetry extend beyond message content. Communication metadata can reveal relationships, patterns of activity and the use of particular services even when application content is unavailable. The Internet Architecture Board's privacy guidance identifies correlation, surveillance and stored-data compromise as relevant concerns and recommends considering data minimisation and identifier exposure in protocol and system design. These concerns are significant for organisations that operate networks supporting employees, customers, service users and connected devices. Network telemetry should consequently be assessed not only for its security value but also for the identifiers and behavioural information it retains.
[10] For Nigerian organisations, this issue arises within a data-protection environment shaped by the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023. The Act provides the national statutory context for the processing of personal data, while the Nigeria Data Protection Commission serves as the relevant regulatory authority. This study does not provide a legal compliance assessment. Instead, it examines a technical approach that may inform organisational efforts to design monitoring processes with greater attention to the exposure of identifying information. Whether a specific telemetry field constitutes personal data, and what legal obligations apply to its processing, depend on the context and applicable law; those questions cannot be resolved by a laboratory experiment alone.
[11] Pseudonymisation and data minimisation offer two complementary approaches. Pseudonymisation replaces selected identifiers with substitute values, potentially reducing direct exposure while preserving some ability to correlate events. Minimisation removes fields or records that are not required for a defined monitoring purpose. Aggregation can further reduce detail by summarising events over a specified time interval. These operations have different effects. Pseudonymisation may preserve linkability and can increase storage size when replacement tokens are longer than original identifiers. Minimisation can reduce the quantity of retained information, while aggregation may remove event-level detail needed for certain investigations. A defensible design must therefore evaluate the stages separately rather than assume that every privacy-oriented transformation reduces data volume or preserves all analytical utility.
[12] This study presents a Privacy-Preserving Network Security Monitoring Framework (PP-NSMF) and evaluates its telemetry transformations in a controlled virtual network. The research question is: How can network security monitoring telemetry be transformed through pseudonymisation, data minimisation and temporal aggregation to reduce exposure of identifying information while retaining selected security-monitoring observations in resource-constrained organisational environments?
[13] The study has three objectives: first, to implement a repeatable pipeline for transforming network telemetry through four defined stages; second, to measure source-matched telemetry byte-volume changes and validate specified identifier-handling properties across six synthetic traffic scenarios; and third, to identify the limitations and operational trade-offs that should inform further evaluation. The contribution is an implemented proof of concept and an auditable experimental method, rather than a claim that the framework has established comprehensive privacy protection or general detection effectiveness.
[14] 2. Literature Review and Conceptual Background
[15] 2.1 Network Telemetry as a Privacy Problem
[16] Network security monitoring depends on telemetry that describes communications, connections and protocol activity. Although such records support incident investigation and threat analysis, they can also expose network configurations, communication relationships and behavioural patterns. Van Dijkhuizen and van der Ham's survey of network traffic anonymisation techniques demonstrates that protecting captured traffic has long been a challenge for research and operational network analysis. Their review examines available techniques and implementations and highlights the importance of evaluating how anonymisation methods affect the information retained in network traces.
[17] This concern extends beyond conventional packet capture. Pan et al. investigated privacy and security problems arising when plaintext in-band network telemetry is reported from the data plane for analysis in the control plane. They proposed a privacy-preserving system using homomorphic encryption, compression, integrity verification and anomaly analysis over encrypted telemetry, and evaluated the implementation in an IP-over-elastic-optical-network testbed. Their work demonstrates that privacy-preserving monitoring can be approached through changes to how telemetry is protected and analysed, rather than treating the disclosure of detailed monitoring records as unavoidable.
[18] These studies establish the relevance of protecting network telemetry, but they also illustrate different technical approaches. Encryption protects data against unauthorised disclosure under specified conditions, whereas pseudonymisation, field minimisation and aggregation change the representation or content of retained records. PP-NSMF focuses on the latter operations. Its central question is how these transformations affect the size and identifying content of monitoring telemetry, rather than how to perform analytics directly on encrypted data.
[19] 2.2 Pseudonymisation, Anonymisation and Linkability
[20] Network trace anonymisation research highlights a persistent tension between reducing information leakage and retaining sufficient structure for security analysis. Mohammady et al. examined this problem in A Multi-view Approach to Preserve Privacy and Utility in Network Trace Anonymization. They observed that conventional approaches, including prefix-preserving IP address pseudonymisation, may remain vulnerable to adversaries with prior knowledge of network flows or may require sanitisation that reduces analytical utility. Their proposed approach uses multiple anonymised views to improve privacy while preserving access to useful analysis results. Experiments using real network traces showed lower information leakage than the comparison approach while maintaining comparable utility.
[21] More recent research also demonstrates that anonymisation does not necessarily prevent traffic correlation. Fiore and Palmieri proposed a distance-based framework for associating network activity observed at different points in anonymising overlay networks. Their experiments illustrate how patterns in traffic traces may permit associations even when direct origin information is obscured. Although their work addresses overlay-network traffic correlation rather than organisational log transformation, it reinforces the broader point that removing or obscuring direct identifiers does not eliminate every route to linkage.
[22] The implications for PP-NSMF are twofold. First, replacing an IP address with a pseudonym should not be described as equivalent to anonymisation. Second, pseudonymisation must be evaluated according to the properties it is intended to provide. The present study uses HMAC-SHA-256-based pseudonyms and checks mapping consistency and the absence of specified raw identifiers in transformed outputs. These tests evaluate defined implementation properties; they do not constitute a comprehensive assessment of re-identification resistance. Consistent pseudonyms may preserve useful event correlation, but that same property can enable linkage within the relevant scope.
[23] 2.3 Data Minimisation, Aggregation and Analytical Utility
[24] Pseudonymisation alone does not determine how much information a monitoring system retains. Field and record selection can remove data that are unnecessary for a defined security purpose, while aggregation can replace individual observations with summaries over a specified interval. These operations may reduce stored telemetry, but their consequences depend on which fields and events are retained and what analysts subsequently need to investigate.
[25] Research on configurable network-flow anonymisation directly addresses the relationship between privacy and intrusion-detection utility. A 2024 study in Computers & Electrical Engineering proposed a configurable approach that permits different anonymity levels for different traffic classes and examines the trade-off between privacy preservation and the utility of data used by machine-learning-based network intrusion-detection systems. This line of research is relevant because it treats privacy and analytical utility as jointly important design considerations rather than assuming that greater transformation is always preferable.
[26] However, byte-volume reduction is not itself a measure of analytical utility. A smaller dataset may still retain the information needed for a particular task, or it may remove evidence required to correlate events and reconstruct an incident. Similarly, aggregation can reduce the granularity of individual records without necessarily eliminating every identifying pattern. The utility of transformed telemetry must therefore be evaluated against defined monitoring tasks rather than inferred from file size alone.
[27] PP-NSMF separates three processing operations: pseudonymisation at C1, minimisation with pseudonymisation at C2, and minimisation with pseudonymisation followed by 60-second aggregation at C3. This staged design makes it possible to distinguish the representation overhead of pseudonymisation from the volume changes associated with subsequent field and record selection and aggregation.
[28] 2.4 Research Gap and Contribution
[29] The reviewed literature establishes that network telemetry can expose sensitive information, that anonymisation may be vulnerable to correlation, and that privacy transformations can affect the utility of security analysis. Existing studies have explored sophisticated network-trace anonymisation, encrypted telemetry analytics and configurable privacy–utility trade-offs. These contributions provide important foundations, but they do not remove the need for implementation-level evaluation of simpler, staged telemetry transformations in specific monitoring environments.
[30] The contribution of PP-NSMF is an auditable experimental evaluation of pseudonymisation, minimisation and temporal aggregation applied to telemetry from six controlled network-traffic scenarios. It measures source-matched Suricata byte-volume changes, separately records event counts, and validates specified identifier-handling and pseudonym-mapping properties. It also distinguishes a configured alert rule firing from general detection effectiveness. The framework is not presented as a new cryptographic primitive or as proof of complete privacy. Rather, it provides a practical method for examining how defined transformations affect telemetry representation and volume while documenting the properties that remain untested.
[31] 3. PP-NSMF Framework and Research Methodology
[32] 3.1 Framework Design
[33] PP-NSMF uses four telemetry stages. C0 represents raw Suricata EVE telemetry. C1 applies pseudonymisation to selected identifiers. C2 applies field and record minimisation while retaining pseudonymisation. C3 aggregates the minimised, pseudonymised data into 60-second windows. The sequence permits examination of the effect of each processing stage rather than treating the complete pipeline as a single transformation.
[34] The implementation used a controlled GNS3 virtual network with a client, server, attacker and monitoring nodes. Zeek and Suricata observed the synthetic traffic. Python scripts generated workloads, transformed telemetry, validated selected identifier properties and calculated the reported metrics. The Suricata byte-volume comparison uses suricata/eve.json as the raw baseline because this is the source file transformed into the corresponding C1, C2 and C3 outputs. Zeek logs were processed separately and were not combined with Suricata to calculate a single reduction percentage.
[35] 3.2 Experimental Scenarios and Procedure
[36] Six scenarios were implemented:
[37] S1 — HTTP requests: five HTTP GET requests and corresponding successful responses.
[38] S2 — DNS queries: six queries comprising three A and three AAAA queries.
[39] S3 — controlled TCP scan: connection attempts against five specified ports, without application payload.
[40] S4 — authentication failures: five synthetic HTTP Basic-authentication attempts using deliberately invalid passwords against a laboratory endpoint.
[41] S5 — periodic connections: four payload-free TCP connections generated at regular intervals.
[42] S6 — synthetic bulk transfer: a 65,536-byte transfer consisting of deterministic NUL bytes to a laboratory receiver.
[43] Each scenario was repeated five times, except S2, for which four compliant runs were included. The legacy S2/R1 run was excluded because it used a different HMAC key, a legacy transformation script and an unclean capture boundary. The first S4/R1 attempt was excluded because validation found a raw-identifier leak in a nested flow field; the failed attempt was preserved, the transformation was corrected and the successful retry was included. The resulting compliant dataset contained 29 successful runs.
[44] Raw captures and transformed outputs were retained. The evidence inventory used SHA-256 checksums, and the reported audit confirmed 243 checksum entries with no failures. Transformation versions differed by scenario group: 2.0.0 for S1 and compliant S2, 2.1.0 for S3, and 2.2.0 for S4–S6. The validator was version 1.1.0, and the compliant runs used the same recorded HMAC key identifier.
[45] 3.3 Metrics and Validation
[46] For source-matched Suricata byte-volume comparisons, reduction was calculated as:
[47] Reduction (%) = 100 × (1 − Transformed Bytes / Raw Bytes)
[48] The denominator is the size of the corresponding run's raw suricata/eve.json file; the numerator is the size of its C1, C2 or C3 output. Byte sizes were measured as file sizes. This metric describes telemetry byte-volume change only. It does not directly measure privacy risk, identifier exposure, analytical utility or detection effectiveness.
[49] Record counts were analysed separately because record selection and field removal affect counts and byte sizes differently. Zeek byte-size changes were excluded from claims of pure data minimisation because the transformation rewrites log headers, confounding the comparison. Validation examined whether specified raw identifiers remained in transformed outputs and whether pseudonym mappings were consistent and injective where applicable. These checks were limited to the identifiers, fields and mappings implemented in the validator; they were not a formal proof of anonymity.
[50] The experiment was descriptive. It did not use labelled ground truth to calculate detection precision, recall, false-positive rate or false-negative rate. S4's configured HTTP-401 rule was evaluated for rule firing, not general detection performance. Investigation utility was not measured.
[51] 4. Results
[52] 4.1 Telemetry byte-volume changes
[53] The corrected source-matched Suricata results show that C2 byte-volume reduction varied across scenarios. Mean C2 reduction was 95.46% for S1, 85.78% for S2, 95.26% for S3, 87.78% for S4, 96.34% for S5 and 98.30% for S6. C3 reduction was 99.23%, 98.94%, 98.95%, 98.88%, 99.18% and 98.47%, respectively.
[54] These values indicate that the minimisation and pseudonymisation stage substantially reduced the size of the selected Suricata telemetry relative to the raw source file in this laboratory configuration. The additional C3 aggregation stage reduced the output further under the defined 60-second procedure. The values should not be interpreted as directly comparable measures of privacy protection across scenarios, because the workloads and source event structures differ.
[55] The largest reported C2 reduction was observed in S6, at 98.30%, while S2 had the lowest, at 85.78%. These differences reflect the combination of event selection, retained field structure and workload-specific telemetry. The experiment does not isolate each factor sufficiently to attribute the difference to workload alone.
[56] 4.2 Pseudonymisation overhead
[57] C1 byte-volume reduction was negative in every compliant run. Scenario-mean C1 reduction ranged from −0.14% (S6) to −2.28% (S4); across all 29 individual runs, per-run values ranged from −0.09% to −2.29%. This means that C1 outputs were larger than the raw Suricata baseline. The implementation used HMAC-derived pseudonym tokens that were longer than the original IPv4 addresses. Replacing identifiers therefore increased the number of bytes even though it changed their representation.
[58] This result is important because it separates the purpose of pseudonymisation from storage optimisation. C1 was not designed to reduce the number of bytes; it was designed to replace selected identifiers while retaining the fields required at that stage. Its negative reduction values are consistent with that design. A system that evaluates privacy transformations only by file size could incorrectly classify this stage as unsuccessful.
[59] 4.3 Record counts and aggregation
[60] Record counts changed separately from byte sizes. Mean C0 and C2 Suricata record counts were 17 and 5 for S1; 17.25 and 12 for S2; 7 and 5 for S3; 22 and 15 for S4; 6.2 and 4 for S5; and 2.4 and 1 for S6. Each compliant run produced one C3 aggregation window.
[61] The fractional scenario means for S2, S5 and S6 reflect variation in the number of raw source events across runs, not fractional records within individual runs. For example, some runs contained an additional Suricata statistics event because of emission timing. This variation also affected raw file sizes and the resulting reduction percentages. The evidence does not indicate that the intended workload changed in those runs.
[62] The record-count results show that minimisation removed selected records as well as fields. Byte-volume reduction captures both the reduction in records and changes to the size of retained records. These measures must therefore remain distinct.
[63] 4.4 Privacy-related validation
[64] All 29 compliant runs passed the implemented raw-identifier absence checks, and all 29 reported successful pseudonym-linkability validation where applicable. The checks examined specified IP addresses and scenario-specific identifiers such as the test DNS domain and HTTP hostname. C3 and some minimised Zeek outputs were marked not applicable for mapping consistency where identifiers had been removed by the relevant transformation.
[65] These results demonstrate that the tested outputs satisfied the implemented validation conditions. They do not prove that every potentially identifying attribute was removed, that re-identification is impossible, or that the framework provides anonymity. The scope of the conclusion is limited by the identifier inventory and field checks implemented in the validator.
[66] 4.5 Observed events and detection scope
[67] The experiment recorded the intended traffic observations for the six scenarios. S1 produced five HTTP requests and five Suricata HTTP events per run. S2 produced six DNS queries and twelve Suricata DNS events per run. S3 produced five scan connections and five flow events. S4 produced five authentication attempts and five HTTP events. S5 produced four periodic connections and four flow events. S6 produced one bulk-transfer connection per run, with Zeek recording the 65,536-byte application payload.
[68] S4 generated five alerts per successful run because the configured Suricata HTTP-401 rule matched the deliberately generated responses. This confirms that the rule loaded and fired for the known pattern. It does not establish general detection effectiveness because the experiment did not measure false positives and false negatives against labelled ground truth. S3, S5 and S6 were run without alert rules and therefore produced flow observations rather than detection results.
[69] 5. Discussion
[70] 5.1 Interpreting the transformation trade-off
[71] The results show why pseudonymisation, minimisation and aggregation should be evaluated as distinct operations. Pseudonymisation changed the representation of selected identifiers but increased byte volume. Minimisation reduced the set of retained fields and records, producing substantial C2 reductions. Aggregation further reduced the representation into one window per run. This sequence demonstrates that privacy-oriented processing can have different effects on storage size at different stages.
[72] The findings are consistent with the broader principle that privacy engineering requires attention to both the information exposed and the purpose for which it is retained. RFC 6973 recommends considering the identifiers used by systems, the opportunities for correlation and the information necessary to achieve the system's purpose. In this experiment, the HMAC-derived tokens supported consistent pseudonym mapping at C1 and C2, but that consistency also means the tokens remain linkable within the relevant scope. The results therefore support a distinction between reducing direct identifier exposure and eliminating the ability to correlate activity.
[73] 5.2 Implications for monitoring practice
[74] For an organisation implementing a comparable pipeline, the results suggest that minimisation policies should be designed around explicit monitoring objectives. Security teams should determine which event types and fields are needed for incident triage, threat hunting and audit, rather than retain every available field by default. A transformation policy should document which identifiers are replaced, which fields are removed, which events are excluded, and how long pseudonyms remain stable.
[75] The C3 stage also illustrates a trade-off between compact summaries and event-level detail. A 60-second aggregate can reduce the retained representation, but the experiment did not test whether an analyst could reconstruct incidents from these summaries. The framework therefore cannot yet claim that the resulting telemetry preserves adequate investigation utility. A future evaluation should test representative analyst tasks or defined investigation questions before asserting that the utility–privacy balance has been achieved.
[76] 5.3 Relevance to Nigerian organisations
[77] The framework may be relevant to Nigerian organisations that need to retain security telemetry while reducing unnecessary exposure of identifying information. It offers an implementation pattern that can be examined using open-source monitoring tools and controlled workloads. Its relevance is particularly practical as a design and evaluation approach, not as a turnkey compliance solution.
[78] The Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023 provides the relevant statutory context for organisational personal-data processing. However, this study did not perform a legal analysis of any organisation's processing activities, establish lawful bases, assess data-subject rights, or test governance controls. A deployment would require context-specific decisions about purposes, access, retention, key custody, accountability and the legal status of the processed data. Technical transformations can support such governance but cannot substitute for it.
[79] 6. Limitations and Future Research
[80] This study has several limitations. First, all workloads were synthetic and generated in a controlled virtual network. The results may not represent traffic diversity, operational constraints or monitoring requirements in production networks. Second, the compliant dataset contained five repetitions per scenario except S2, which contained four. These repetitions support assessment of consistency within the experimental setup but do not establish broad reproducibility across environments.
[81] Third, the identifier validator checked a defined set of fields and values. It did not conduct a comprehensive re-identification or adversarial analysis. Fourth, the study measured telemetry byte volume and record counts but did not measure investigation utility. It also did not evaluate general detection effectiveness using labelled ground truth. The S4 alert result is limited to configured rule firing on known HTTP-401 responses. S3, S5 and S6 had no alert rules configured, so their flow observations are not detection-performance results and should not be read as evidence of absent threats.
[82] Finally, Zeek byte-volume comparisons were excluded from pure reduction claims because log-header rewriting confounded the interpretation. Future work should evaluate the framework using more diverse traffic, broader identifier inventories, controlled adversarial tests, measured analyst tasks, retention and access-control policies, and detectors evaluated against labelled data. Tests in operational environments would be needed before generalising the findings to Nigerian organisations.
[83] 7. Conclusion
[84] This study asked how network security monitoring telemetry can be transformed through pseudonymisation, data minimisation and temporal aggregation to reduce the exposure of identifying information while retaining selected security-monitoring observations. The implemented PP-NSMF pipeline answers this question for the six scenarios tested: staged transformation substantially reduced telemetry byte volume at C2 and C3, while pseudonymisation alone did not. Across 29 compliant runs from six synthetic traffic scenarios, the corrected source-matched Suricata results showed C2 byte-volume reductions of 85.78%–98.30% and C3 reductions of 98.47%–99.23%. Pseudonymisation alone increased byte volume in every run, demonstrating that identifier protection and storage reduction are different objectives. The implemented validation checks reported no raw-identifier leakage and consistent pseudonym mappings in all 29 runs, within the checks' defined scope.
[85] The findings demonstrate the feasibility of the implemented pipeline in a controlled laboratory setting, but they do not prove complete privacy, legal compliance, adequate investigation utility or general detection effectiveness. PP-NSMF should therefore be treated as a proof of concept and an auditable basis for further evaluation. The next stage is to test whether the transformed telemetry supports defined security tasks while measuring privacy-related properties under more realistic workloads and organisational constraints.
